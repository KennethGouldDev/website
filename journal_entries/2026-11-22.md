---
title: If you don't heal from victimhood you become the abuser
date: November 22, 2026
---

When you go through a traumatic event, your brain automatically tries to figure it out. It does this whether you like it or not.<br>
When something bad happens to you, your brain wants a variety of things. It wants closure, it wants a reason this happened, it wants to figure out how to not let this happen again. <br>
Your brain does this because it wants to keep you safe. If it can figure out *why* something happened, especially if it's bad, then you would know how to prevent it. Thus you are more likely to not go through the same thing again. <br>
And when you look around, it's easy to notice this. It happens not just with big things, but with small things too. If you trip on a rock, the next time you cross that same path, you're gonna look out for that rock so you don't trip over it again. This is an easy example because it's tangible and physical. However, when it comes to mental pain, things get a little more complicated and harder to spot. <br>
One of the biggest differences between mental and physical pain, is that avoiding physical pain is of course (immediately) not painful at all. It's not painful to be like 'oh wait, i dont want to trip over that rock again' and step to the side of it. However, when working through mental pain, it *is* painful. In order to work through a mentally traumatic event, you have to revisit it. You have to think about how you were feeling in those moments, you have to think about how the other person was feeling, how you ended up in that situation, and what made the other person do that. <br>
Not only do you have to think of all of this, chances are, thinking of it this deeply is making you kind of re-experience the event, AND you have to work hard to make sure you're revisiting it in a healthy way, and that you're drawing an optimistic conclusion. <br>
This is incredibly difficult! That's why people go to school for a psychology degree, and try to help people with this problem by listening and guiding them for hours. And sometimes, it *still* doesnt work lol. <br>
In this situation, you can either take control of what your brain is doing, or just kinda coast and let what happens happen. However, of course, not doing this very important work can come with a very detrimental outcome. <br>
*Suprisingly*, **Your brain is actually gonna work through it anyway**. <br>
But it's not gonna be *your* doing, it's gonna be your subconcious. And if you haven't conciously worked through this kind of thing before, your subconcious is just gonna try to deal with it in the way that it knows, and usually in the easiest way possible. <br>
The closure that your brain wants, it will try to get that closure through any means necessary -- to the point of even fabricating a false world and false beliefs in order to make you feel safe again. <br>
Lets say someone got hurt by someone else a lot. For someone with an untrained mind, the path of least resistence in dealing with this kind of pain, usually looks like this: <br><br>
What happened to me? -> they hurt you <br>
Why did they do that? -> because they are evil <br>
How do i not let this happen again? -> don't involve yourself with them<br>
Why? -> because they are evil and they will hurt you again <br>
<br>
This is the simplest chain of thought your mind will probably come up with. The thing is, if the person is actually evil, then this works fine. So what's the problem here? <br>
The problem here is that the thinking is way too shallow, and because of that, it's really easy for a thought here to be accepted without being questioned. And when beliefs are accepted without being questioned, thats a how a false (and usually negative) belief gets implanted into the mind. <br>
Instead, the person should ideally question as many steps in this line of thinking as possible. For example, answering to the question "What happened to me?" with "they hurt you" makes it way more black and white than it probably actually is. The statement "they hurt you" comes with the insinuation that the person actually intended to hurt you. What if the other person was actually doing something that they thought was thoughtful, but simply because of a cultural or circumstantial difference, you misinterpreted what they meant? <br>
What if instead of just avoiding them and not involving yourself with them, having a simple conversation about how they made you feel would clear everything up? Not only could that conversation verify if they actually intended to hurt you, if it goes well then you've avoided the bad thing happening again, you will probably grow a deeper connection, and you'll both be happy. <br>
There's something even bigger at play here though, which gets me to the title of the journal. <br>
The example that I used is of a Person A hurting Person B, and Person B taking in a new belief that they think the Person A is evil and that they should avoid them. <br>
The thing is, having this kind of belief actually *seeps into* the other beliefs that Person B has. Having the belief that someone hurt them, and knowing how that pain made them feel, will make them more on the lookout of a similar pain happening again. <br>
They know that evil people hurt them, but actually, how are they supposed to know who is evil? Hmm, maybe they should err on the side of caution, because what if they get close to someone and they get hurt again? <br>
Now whenever they see someone, part of their mind is on the look-out, with their defenses up, looking for and almost expecting them to be evil, ready to protect themselves if they get hurt. Day by day, as this belief of people being evil sits in their mind untouched, it starts to take over slowly and slowly even more. <br>
All of a sudden, they think the whole world is evil. Everyone who gets close to them secretly wants to hurt them, and they don't really have a concrete reason *why* they know this... they just kind of... *know* they will get hurt. <br>
But you can't really avoid people. And of course, people make them feel less lonely, and they don't want to feel lonely. So, they *do* want people around, but, they also don't want people to get *too* close... <br>
But of course someone is going to get close to them. And when someone does, their mind kind of freaks out. <br>
This person is close to me, they're gonna hurt me! What do I do? Oh, well, a way I can defend myself, is *i* can yell at them first before I *know* they are gonna yell at *me*. If I defend myself by hurting them first, then it will be harder for them to hurt me. <br> <br>
And that's how it happens. It started with someone hurting them, being inflicted a real pain that deserved being noticed and addressed. <br>
However, it was just too difficult and they didn't work through it. Their brain did it for them anyway, and concluded with a small but dangerous belief that some people were just evil, and that virus festered in their mind long enough to grow. <br>
When you have the belief that the world is out to get you, it becomes justified that hurting the world is okay, because it's evil anyway. <br>
Not only did they become a victim to their own mind, but they even passed the trauma onto someone else, and they became the abuser.
