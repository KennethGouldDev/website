title: if you don't heal from victimhood you become the abuser
date: November 22, 2026
---

If you go through a traumatic event, your brain will automatically try to figure things out. 
It does this whether you like it or not.
When something bad happens to you, your brain wants a variety of things. It wants closure, it wants a *reason* why this happened, and it wants to figure out how to not let this happen again. 
Your brain does this because it wants to keep you *safe*. If it can figure out *why* something happened, *especially* if it's bad, then you will probably know how to prevent it from happening again. 
And when you look around, it's easy to notice that the brain does this. It happens not just with big things, but with small things too.
If you're walking along a path and you trip on a rock, the next time you cross the same path, you're gonna look out for that rock so you don't trip on it again.
This is an easy example to wrap your head around because it's easily observable and physical. However, when it comes to mental pain, things get a little more complicated. 
One of the biggest differences between mental and physical pain, is that avoiding physical pain is of course (immediately) not painful at all. It's not that painful to be like "oh wait, i dont want to trip over that rock again!" and step to side to avoid it. 
However, when working through mental pain, it *is* painful. In order to work through a mentally traumatic event, you have to revisit it. You have to think about how you were feeling in that moment, you have to think about how the other person was feeling, how you ended up in that situation, etc. 
Not only do you have to think of all of this, but chances are, thinking of it this deeply is making you kind of re-experience the event. On top of that, you have to work hard to make sure you're revisiting it in a healthy way, and that you're drawing an optimistic conclusion. 
*This is incredibly difficult!* That's why people go to school for a psychology degree, try to help people with this problem by listening and guiding them for hours, and sometimes it *still* doesnt work lol. 
Finding yourself in this type of situation, you can either take control of what your brain is doing, or just kinda coast and let what happens happen. However, of course, if you decide to coast, choosing to not conciously do this very important work can come with a very detrimental outcome. 
*Suprisingly*, **Your brain is actually gonna work through it anyway**. 
But it's not gonna be *your* doing, it's gonna be your *subconcious*. And if you haven't conciously worked through this kind of thing before, your subconcious is just gonna try to deal with it by taking the path of least resistance. 
The closure that your brain wants, it will try to get that closure through any means necessary -- to the point of even fabricating a false world and false beliefs in order to make you feel safe again. 
Lets say someone got hurt by someone else a lot. For someone with an untrained mind, the path of least resistence in dealing with this kind of pain, usually looks like this: 

What happened to me? -> they hurt you 
Why did they do that? -> because they are evil 
How do i not let this happen again? -> don't involve yourself with them
Why? -> evil people will hurt you again 

This is the simplest chain of thought your mind will probably come up with. The thing is, if the person is actually evil, then this works fine. So what's the problem here? 
The problem here is that the thinking is way too shallow, and because of that, it's really easy for a thought here to be accepted without being questioned. And when beliefs are accepted without being questioned, thats a how a false (and usually negative) belief gets implanted into the mind. 
Instead, the person should ideally question as many steps in this line of thinking as possible. For example, answering to the question "What happened to me?" with "they hurt you" makes it seem way more black and white than it probably actually is. The statement "they hurt you" comes with the insinuation that the person actually intended to hurt you. What if the other person was actually doing something that they thought was thoughtful, but simply because of a cultural or circumstantial difference, you misinterpreted what they intended? 
What if instead of just avoiding them, having a simple conversation about how they made you feel would clear everything up? Not only could that conversation verify if they actually intended to hurt you, if it goes well then you've avoided the bad thing happening again, and you will probably grow a deeper connection! Lots of alternative and healthy paths open up with just a bit of thinking.
There's something even bigger at play here though, which gets me to the title of the journal. 
The example that I used is of a Person A hurting Person B, and Person B taking in a new belief that they think the Person A is evil and that they should avoid them. 
The thing is, beliefs are funny and are a very integrated component of our minds. Most beliefs kind of *seep* into the other beliefs that we have. Person B, having the belief that someone hurt them, and knowing how that pain made them feel, will make them construct more beliefs that keep them on the lookout of a similar pain happening again. 
They know that evil people hurt them, but actually, how are they supposed to know who is evil? Hmm, maybe they should err on the side of caution, because what if they get close to someone and they get hurt again? 
Now whenever they see someone, part of their mind is on the look-out, defenses up almost expecting them to be evil, ready to protect themselves if they get hurt. Day by day, as this belief of people being evil sits in their mind untouched, it starts to take over slowly and slowly even more. 
All of a sudden, they might end up thinking the whole world is evil. Everyone who gets close to them secretly wants to hurt them, and to them they don't really have a concrete reason *why* they know this... they just kind of... really feel and *know* they will get hurt. 
But yknow, you can't really avoid people. 
And of course, being around people makes them feel less lonely, and they don't want to feel lonely.
So, they *do* want people around, but, they also don't want people to get *too* close... they can't afford to be vulnerable or else they will get hurt again.
But of course, inevitably someone is going to get close to them. And when someone does, their mind kind of freaks out. 
This person is close to me, they're gonna hurt me! What do I do? Oh, well, a way I can defend myself, is.. uh.. if *I* can yell at them *first* before *they* yell at *me*, I can soften the blow. I can defend myself by not getting hit first. If *I* hurting them *first*, then it will be harder for *them* to hurt **me**.  
And that's how it can happen. It starts with someone hurting them, being inflicted a real pain that deserves being noticed and addressed, 
However, it was just too difficult and they didn't work through it. They didn't work through it consciously, so their brain did it for them anyway. With that they drew a conclusion, with a small but dangerous belief that some people were just evil. Without addressing it, that virus festered in their mind long enough to grow. 
When you have the belief that the world is out to get you, it becomes justified that hurting the world is okay, because it's evil anyway. 
Not only did they become a victim to their own mind, but they even passed the trauma onto someone else, and they became the abuser.
